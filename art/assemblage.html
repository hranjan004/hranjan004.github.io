Container Assemblage

An orientation bag, a watch, a wheel and headphones become a study of arrival, time and movement. Inspired by the associations objects carry, I used the container to hold a small narrative about entering a new place.

Reflection

The bag I chose as my container is the first thing I received at UC Santa Cruz on my orientation day. It is not a neutral object. It came loaded with the promise of a new beginning, handed to me by an institution welcoming me into a place I did not yet know how to inhabit.

Using it as the foundation of this assemblage felt necessary precisely because of that tension between what the bag was supposed to represent and what my actual experience of that time looked and felt like.

Inside the bag I placed a watch that belongs to my father. I took it from him in high school, and carrying it into this new chapter of my life was something I did without fully understanding why. For this piece, the watch represents lost time, a strange sleepwalking quality that defined much of my first period at Santa Cruz. I was present physically but not truly internalizing what was happening around me. I was moving through days without absorbing them, too disoriented or perhaps too afraid to let experiences land. The watch, hidden inside the bag rather than displayed on the surface, captures that quality of time that was passing without being felt. It is there, it is real, but you have to look for it.

The wheel I included speaks to the constant movement that has characterized my life. Even when I felt lost or frozen internally, things kept moving forward. The wheel does not ask permission to turn. It was important to me that this object exist in relationship to the watch because together they describe something true about that period, time moving, life moving, and me somewhat detached from both.

The headphones represent music and the particular kind of isolation that music can offer.

When I arrived somewhere new and unfamiliar, music became a private world I could return to. It was comfort that did not require explanation or social navigation. Writing served a similar function. The ideas and thoughts I was constantly sitting with had nowhere else to go, and so they went onto the page. Including a reference to writing in the assemblage felt important because it is how I processed what I could not yet speak out loud.

Joseph Cornell's approach to assemblage influenced this work most directly. Cornell built boxes that held objects not for their decorative value but for their psychological weight, their capacity to suggest longing, displacement, and private emotional worlds. That is what I was reaching for.

The bag functions conceptually as both exterior and interior self. On the outside it is an institutional welcome, a clean start. On the inside it holds the quieter and more complicated truth of what that start actually felt like. The arrangement moves from surface to depth deliberately, asking the viewer to look past the obvious gesture of arrival and into something more unresolved and honest.
