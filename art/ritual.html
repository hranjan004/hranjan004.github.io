Ritual Tool

A tissue-and-glue impression of a comb turns an ordinary morning action into something visible. Reproducing its closely spaced teeth made a familiar object unexpectedly difficult, and made me look more carefully at its form.

Artist statement

My work begins with routine and the unspoken reverence we assign to everyday objects. Through paper lamination and layered tissue, I developed a sculptural impression that is fragile yet surprisingly sturdy. A comb, something so ordinary it barely registers, emerged from dozens of patient layers of toilet paper that captured every tooth and ridge of its deceptively complex surface. This piece explores ritual and embodied knowledge, interacting with space by making a private morning habit visible and tangible. I became interested in how much meaning lives inside repetition, how combing my hair each morning before class is less about appearance and more about grounding myself before stepping into the day. The relationship between routine and identity feels endlessly worth exploring.

Reflection

The tool I chose for this project was a comb, something so ordinary that it barely registers as significant in the flow of a day. And yet, for me, it carries a quiet weight that I had not fully articulated until this assignment asked me to slow down and look at it differently.

The comb is part of a small ritual I perform each morning just before I leave for class. There is something deliberate about it. I pick it up, run it through my hair, and in doing so I feel myself settle. It is a moment of focus and concentration, a way of grounding myself before stepping into the demands of the day ahead. It sounds almost too simple to matter, but that brief, repetitive act functions as a kind of threshold between rest and readiness. I am not just fixing my appearance. I am leveling myself.

When I began building the impression using toilet paper rolls and layers of tissue, I was immediately confronted with how deceptively complex the comb's form actually is. It is extremely thin, with closely spaced teeth that run along almost its entire length. Capturing that with tissue required patience and a lot of material. The toilet paper gave the final shell a rough, almost fibrous texture, which contrasted interestingly with how smooth and utilitarian the original comb feels in hand. The thinness of the comb meant the tissue had to be applied carefully to avoid losing the shape altogether. What emerged was a structure that felt simultaneously fragile and strangely sturdy, tethered together by all those layers building on one another.

The impression process made me aware of how much surface area a comb actually has and how all of that surface exists in service of one narrow, repetitive motion. It also made the ritual feel more visible, more real, as though the object had finally earned the significance I had always quietly given it.
