The Enduring Struggle for Liberty

Lincoln’s shepherd-and-wolf metaphor and South Carolina’s secession declaration reveal incompatible meanings of liberty. I argue that freedom is shaped through conflict, collective struggle and changing ideas of whose rights count.

THE QUESTIONWhose freedom does a definition of liberty protect?

Collected Essays · pages 14–15. Original essay text; formatting adapted for the web. Historical claims and interpretations reflect the writing’s original context.

During the Civil War, in a moment that would echo through American history, Lincoln stood before a crowd in Baltimore and shared a powerful metaphor that still resonates today. He spoke of a shepherd protecting sheep from a wolf, noting how the same act could be seen as liberation by one and oppression by another ("Address at Baltimore Sanitary Fair," PRESIDENCY.UCSB.EDU). This vivid imagery wasn't just clever rhetoric – it was Lincoln grappling with the complex nature of liberty itself.

Just a few years earlier, South Carolina had painted a very different picture of liberty in their Declaration of Secession. Their vision was crystal clear but narrowly focused: liberty meant a state's right to maintain its institutions, particularly slavery ("The Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession," AVON.LAW.YALE.EDU). The contrast between these two perspectives reveals a fundamental truth about freedom – while some see it as a living, breathing concept, others try to cast it in stone.

What makes Lincoln's approach particularly compelling is his willingness to embrace liberty's inherent complexity. Through his shepherd-wolf metaphor, he acknowledges a truth that still challenges us today: one person's freedom can feel like another's chains. This wasn't just philosophical musing – it was a direct response to the rigid stance taken by South Carolina's leaders, who viewed liberty solely through the lens of preserving their way of life and property rights, including the ownership of human beings.

The struggle for liberty wasn't just theoretical – it played out in the lives of those who fought for it. As the reference text reminds us, the demands for freedom gained recognition only through constant struggle, from slave revolts to the ultimate crucible of the Civil War. This history shows us that liberty isn't granted from above – it's claimed through persistent effort and sacrifice.

Reading these historical documents today, I'm struck by how they illuminate an essential truth: liberty isn't a fixed concept but a living idea that evolves with society. While South Carolina tried to freeze liberty in time, Lincoln's perspective acknowledged its dynamic nature. This fundamental difference in understanding freedom continues to shape our discussions today.

Perhaps the most valuable lesson we can draw from this historical moment is that liberty will always be in flux, shaped by ongoing dialogue between competing visions of freedom. Rather than seeking a single, universal definition, we might do better to embrace this perpetual evolution as a strength rather than a weakness. After all, isn't the ability to redefine and expand our understanding of liberty part of what makes human progress possible?

In this way, Lincoln's metaphor serves as a powerful reminder that we're all participants in this ongoing conversation about what freedom means. Sometimes we're the sheep, sometimes the wolf, and occasionally we might find ourselves in the role of the shepherd – but always, we're part of this continuing story of how liberty evolves to meet the needs of each new generation. The goal isn't to settle on one unchanging definition but to create a framework that can grow and adapt, ensuring dignity and justice for all.
