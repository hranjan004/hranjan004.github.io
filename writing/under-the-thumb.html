Under the Thumb of the Year

Flood, famine and fire become more than disasters in these myths: they produce explanations, duties and institutions. Through Gilgamesh, the Epic of Creation, Ovid and Demeter, I trace how awe can harden into authority.

THE QUESTIONHow does catastrophe become an institution?

Collected Essays · pages 19–22. Original essay text; formatting adapted for the web. Historical claims and interpretations reflect the writing’s original context. The collection notes that its Ovid quotations use the More translation; they have not been silently relabeled as McCarter.

I keep coming back to one stubborn fact about being human. I want an explanation for everything. Not because I simply like answers, but because the world is too large to face without a story that makes it legible. When nature becomes violent, when flood erases land and famine empties fields and death becomes close enough to feel like a physical presence, my mind refuses to accept that scale as meaningless. The ancient world did not separate ecological observation from divine causation, and that entanglement matters. Without naturalistic frameworks, the most available explanatory language is cosmic and moral. Disaster becomes will. Flood becomes verdict. Famine becomes punishment. Seasons become law. And once nature is narrated as the action of powers greater than humans, humans are narrated into a permanent role. They become subservient worshippers living under forces they cannot control.

I know I am reading as a modern cynic. But the more I sit with the Epic of Creation, Gilgamesh, Ovid's Metamorphoses, and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, the more I feel that my cynicism is not purely imposed from outside. The texts themselves show how catastrophe becomes socially productive. Religion, in these works, is not only private belief. It functions as awe, as cosmology, and as social order. Disaster triggers awe. Awe needs a map. The map turns into rules. The rules turn into institutions. And institutions stabilize hierarchy, because someone always benefits when the population is taught to fear powers beyond itself.

The mechanism is embedded in creation itself. In the Babylonian Epic of Creation, humans are not born free and then disciplined into obedience. They are manufactured for it. After Marduk defeats Tiamat and orders the cosmos, the gods execute Qingu and from his blood Ea creates mankind, "on whom he imposed the service of the gods, and set the gods free" (Epic of Creation 6.33-34). I cannot read that line without feeling the weight of what it means. Humanity is not a gift. It is a labor contract written in divine blood. The gods were tired of working, so they made us to do it for them. Subservience is not a consequence of catastrophe here. It is the reason we exist. And once that origin story circulates in a culture, every institution built on human labor can claim cosmic justification. You were made to serve.

Gilgamesh takes that foundational subservience and shows what happens when a human tries to resist it. In the early tablets, the people of Uruk cry out against Gilgamesh's tyranny, and the gods respond by creating Enkidu, a wild man who will become the king's equal and companion (Gilgamesh I). Even a king's power is checked by divine intervention. The gods make and unmake the conditions of human life. Then after Gilgamesh and Enkidu kill the Bull of Heaven, the gods decree that Enkidu must die. "My friend, why were the great gods in counsel?" Enkidu asks, and the answer is simply that the gods decided (Gilgamesh VII). Mortality is not natural accident. It is divine policy. And when Enkidu dies and Gilgamesh weeps over his body, the most devastating line in the epic arrives. "My friend whom I love has turned to clay. Am I not like him? Will I not lie down, never to rise again, through all eternity?" (Gilgamesh). That is the moment awe is born. Not awe as pretty wonder, but awe as existential scale, death as a force so much greater than the self that it reorganizes consciousness entirely. Gilgamesh is still a king, still half-divine by reputation, yet he cannot command mortality. That is what catastrophe does. It humiliates power.

But Gilgamesh goes further. After the flood in Tablet XI, catastrophe does not merely produce grief. It produces institution. When the survivors offer sacrifice, the gods themselves respond with hunger. They "smelled the sweet savor, and collected like flies over a (sheep) sacrifice" (Gilgamesh XI). That image is devastating in its honesty. Disaster immediately generates ritual exchange as the repair mechanism of the world. Gods need worship, humans need protection, and sacrifice becomes the currency binding them together. Then the goddess Beletili lifts her jeweled necklace and swears she will be "mindful of these days, and never forget them" (Gilgamesh XI). Catastrophe produces memory-culture. Ritual remembrance becomes institutional time. The disaster is over, but the obedience it created is designed to last forever.

When I move to Ovid, I feel the same mechanism expanded from personal fear to civilizational fear. In Metamorphoses Book 1, catastrophe does not arrive randomly. It arrives as consequence. Before the flood, Ovid describes the primordial state where "to naught was given a proper form, in everything was strife, and all was mingled" (Ovid). Then "God, or kindly Nature, ended strife" and separated land from sky and sea from land (Ovid). That ambiguity is remarkable. Even the poet cannot decide whether it was a god or nature that imposed order. When Jove unleashes the waters, the world returns to that primordial illegibility. Land mixes with sea, farmers row where they once plowed, and every boundary that made civilization possible dissolves. Chaos does not just destroy bodies. It destroys orientation. And that is precisely when divine governance feels necessary.

What seals my interpretation is who survives. After the flood erases almost everyone, Jove observes only two people remaining, "both innocent, both worshippers of the gods" (Ovid). Catastrophe becomes a selection mechanism. The human who deserves to exist after disaster is the worshipper. Subservience is linked to survival. Then Ovid pushes the institutional consequence further. Deucalion and Pyrrha cannot restart society by confidence alone. They must consult the oracle of Themis for the method to restore humanity (Ovid). Recovery requires authorized interpretation. Not figuring it out on your own, but asking sacred authority for correct ritual knowledge. And even once the oracle speaks, Pyrrha refuses the literal command to cast her mother's bones. It takes Deucalion to reinterpret the riddle, figuring out that "bones" means stones. Power shifts from physical strength to sacred knowledge. The institution here is interpretive monopoly. After catastrophe, whoever decodes divine speech holds power over reconstruction.

Ovid's Phaethon shows catastrophe from the opposite direction. Where the flood punishes human corruption, the fire of Phaethon punishes human overreach. A mortal boy seizes the reins of the sun chariot, convinced he can do a god's job, and the earth burns (Ovid). Mountains scorch, rivers dry up, and the skin of nations darkens under impossible heat. Zeus must intervene with a thunderbolt to stop the destruction. The message is clear. When a human tries to occupy a divine role, the world itself catches fire. Catastrophe enforces the boundary between mortal and immortal. It teaches you to stay in your place. And the institutional lesson mirrors the flood. After Phaethon's fall, the sun god must be convinced to resume his duty, and the cosmic order is restored only because each being accepts its assigned function. Overreach produces disaster. Obedience produces stability.

If the flood and fire enforce subservience through spectacular destruction, the Homeric Hymn to Demeter shows how famine enforces it through slow, grinding devastation. When Hades abducts Persephone, Demeter does not simply mourn. She weaponizes the earth. She "prevented the earth, the nurturer of many, from sending up any seed for a whole year" (Hymn to Demeter). The bright grain of wheat fell into the ground and the oxen dragged their plows without result. The human race stood on the verge of extinction. But here is what makes this text essential to my argument. The hymn tells us that Demeter's famine threatened not only humans but the gods themselves, who "faced the loss of gifts and sacrifices they received from mortals" (Hymn to Demeter). That mutual dependency is the quiet engine of the entire mythic system. Gods need worship as much as humans need grain. The relationship is not pure domination. It is a binding contract disguised as hierarchy.

And the resolution of that famine is where institution is born. Zeus negotiates. Persephone will spend one third of the year with Hades and two thirds with her mother. The seasons themselves become the permanent scar of a divine negotiation. Every winter is Demeter's grief made structural. Every spring is her reunion. Agriculture, the most fundamental human institution, is locked into the rhythm of a goddess's emotional calendar. And then Demeter commands the Eleusinians to build her a temple and declares "I will myself instruct you in the sacred rites so that, in the future, you may perform the rituals in the proper way" (Hymn to Demeter). Famine produces temple.

Temple produces priesthood. Priesthood produces ritual authority. The pipeline from catastrophe to institution is not my cynical projection. It is narrated explicitly.

When I place these texts beside each other, I see a system. The Epic of Creation establishes that humans were made to serve. Gilgamesh shows mortality humiliating even kings into worship, and the post-flood sacrifice revealing that gods depend on human ritual as much as humans depend on divine protection. Ovid shows catastrophe as both moral enforcement and the birthplace of interpretive authority. Phaethon shows what happens when a mortal forgets his place. The Hymn to Demeter shows famine converting grief into seasonal law and sacred rites. At every stage, the system reinforces itself, because even the gods depend on the worship that catastrophe produces.

My cynicism enters at the final step, and I think the texts invite it. Once catastrophe is framed as divine governance, someone must interpret that governance. Someone must explain why the flood came, why famine persists, what the oracle means, how the rites must be performed. That someone becomes authority. The community's need for meaning becomes the foundation of hierarchy. This does not mean the myths are fake. It means they are effective. They take uncontrollable forces and turn them into an order that can be spoken, enforced, and reproduced. They do not merely describe the world. They build the world they describe.
