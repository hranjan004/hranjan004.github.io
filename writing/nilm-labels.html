When the labels were the problem

Why I replaced LLM-generated NILM labels with physical rules—and why that still does not make every label ground truth.

THE QUESTIONIs the model learning the signal, or repeating the labeler?

Adapted from my Microgrid project records. These are observations from this deployment, not a general comparison of NILM methods.

A model can look convincing while learning almost nothing about the underlying system. In our residential NILM work, all 3,433 sampled heat-pump windows had been labeled OFF by the LLM labeling pipeline. Before trying another architecture, I had to fix the supervision.

Only 2 of 22 appliance outputs had independent measurement. I replaced unreliable labels with physics-informed rules based on sustained behavior, cycling, event shape and temporal constraints. Ambiguous windows were excluded instead of forced into a class.

Panel 3 needed more than a single power threshold. I separated sustained loads, compressor cycles, discrete high-power events and paired pulses. That decomposition helped address repeated false positives by considering how a signal behaves over time.

Architecture comparisons needed the same skepticism. MATNilm produced nearly constant per-appliance guesses, so I retained the BiLSTM on the evidence. A newer model did not solve a supervision problem.

The limitation matters: agreement with a rule-based labeler is not independent appliance accuracy. More independently metered examples remain necessary, especially for rare loads. The lesson I carry forward is to audit labels, inspect model sensitivity and document what an evaluation can actually establish.
