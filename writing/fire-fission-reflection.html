Critical Reflection: Fire and Fission

The companion reflection explains how ancient myths shape the encounter between Prometheus and Oppenheimer. It examines invention as a gift whose consequences escape its maker, and why the screenplay leaves its central disagreement unresolved.

THE QUESTIONWhat responsibility survives after an invention leaves its maker?

Collected Essays · pages 25–27. Original essay text; formatting adapted for the web. Historical claims and interpretations reflect the writing’s original context.

There is a moment in Hesiod's Theogony where the cosmos is still just Chaos. Not disorder exactly but the absence of order. A void waiting for something to fill it. I kept returning to that image when I was writing this screenplay because it felt like the right place to begin a conversation between two figures who each, in their own century, reached into a void and pulled something out that changed everything. Prometheus carried fire down the mountain. Oppenheimer split the atom in the desert. And neither of them could control what happened next. That parallel is the engine of the piece, and the myths we studied in LIT 130 gave me both the language and the architecture to explore it.

The texts that most directly inspired the project were Hesiod's Theogony and the Prometheus tradition from Apollodorus, Ovid's Metamorphoses across several episodes including Deucalion and the flood, Lycaon, Phaethon, Arachne, Medusa, and Cadmus, the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, the Heracles cycle from Apollodorus, and the katabasis tradition running through Vergil's Aeneid Book 6. I did not want these myths to sit in the background as vague allusions. I wanted them in the mouths of the characters, being argued over, being fought about, being interpreted in real time from two fundamentally different positions. The entire dramatic tension of the screenplay rests on the fact that Prometheus and Oppenheimer read the same myths and see completely different things.

This is where the piece engages most directly with the question of nature and myth. One of the recurring concerns of the course has been how ancient cultures used myth to process their relationship with the natural world. Floods, droughts, famine, volcanic eruptions, the movement of the seasons. These were not abstract phenomena to the people who told these stories. They were lived catastrophes that needed explanations and frameworks and meaning. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter does not describe winter as a meteorological event. It describes winter as a mother's grief made planetary. Ovid's Phaethon does not describe climate catastrophe as a matter of atmospheric science. He describes it as a boy reaching for the reins of the sun and losing control. In both cases the natural world is not separate from human and divine emotion. It is an expression of it. The earth goes barren because Demeter mourns. The rivers boil because Phaethon cannot hold the horses. Nature and feeling are the same substance.

What fascinated me was what happens when you put that worldview in conversation with a modern scientific one. Oppenheimer does not think in terms of divine grief or solar chariots. He thinks in terms of chain reactions and blast radii and kilotons. He measures. That is what he says in the screenplay and it is also his limitation. He can quantify the yield of the bomb but he cannot metabolize the meaning of it the way the mythological tradition metabolizes its own disasters. This is Prometheus's strongest argument and it is drawn directly from the course readings. The Trojan War produced the Iliad. Heracles' madness produced the twelve labors. Persephone's descent into the underworld produced the seasons themselves. Every catastrophe in the mythological world is transmuted into story, into meaning, into a framework that helps the species understand what happened to it and grow from it. Prometheus asks Oppenheimer what his bomb produced. What wisdom lives in the interval between the flash and the shadow. And Oppenheimer does not have an answer. Because the bomb does not teach. It only threatens.

But Oppenheimer is not without his own arguments and this is where the reflection between ancient and modern becomes genuinely interesting to me. He turns the myths back on Prometheus not as stories of wisdom but as evidence of instability. Zeus violating Leda and Europa and Danaë. Apollo sending plague into the Greek camp over a personal insult. Artemis demanding the sacrifice of Iphigenia for favorable winds. The entire Trojan War originating from a beauty contest and a golden apple. Oppenheimer reads these myths through a modern lens and sees not a curriculum but a catastrophe. A governance system run by beings with unlimited power and no accountability. And his argument is that the bomb, for all its horror, is the first thing in history that actually imposes a cost on absolute power. Mutually assured destruction as a leash placed around the throat of every potential Zeus.

What the screenplay ultimately tries to reveal is that ancient cultures understood something about the natural world that modern science tends to overlook. They understood that knowledge and consequence are inseparable. The Theogony's succession myth is not just a story about gods overthrowing gods. It is a story about the impossibility of possessing power without being transformed by it. Kronos devours his children because power makes you afraid of what comes next. Zeus overthrows Kronos and becomes a different kind of tyrant. Prometheus steals fire and is chained to a mountain forever. The pattern repeats because the pattern is the point. Power changes the one who holds it and the world that receives it and neither change can be predicted or controlled.

Oppenheimer sits inside that same pattern. He reached into the atom the way Prometheus reached into the hearth. He carried something down from the realm of the inaccessible and placed it in human hands. And now he is on the mountain. Chained not to a rock but to a question. Would you do it again. That question, which Zeus asks Prometheus every morning through the eagle, is the same question the twentieth century asks Oppenheimer through every documentary and every biography and every security hearing transcript. And the answer is the same. Yes. Because the alternative was the mud. Because watching a species grovel under something it could resist was unbearable.

The myths from this course gave me a vocabulary for something I had been thinking about for a long time. How do you talk about the moral weight of invention? How do you hold two truths at once, that fire was necessary and that fire burns, that the bomb ended a war and that the bomb ended two cities? The ancient Greeks did not resolve those contradictions. They dramatized them. They put them in the mouths of gods and heroes and let the audience sit in the discomfort. That is what I tried to do with this screenplay. Not to resolve the argument between Prometheus and Oppenheimer but to let it breathe. To let both of them be right. To let both of them be wrong. And to end not with a verdict but with a question that never stops being asked, on a mountain that never stops being cold, by two figures who gave the world more than it was ready for and who would do it again tomorrow.
