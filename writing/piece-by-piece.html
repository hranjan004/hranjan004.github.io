Piece by Piece

A participatory mural proposal for UCSC’s Barn Theater, built around memory, shared materials and consultation.

THE QUESTIONWho gets to leave a mark on a shared place?

A proposal, not a completed installation. The archive does not establish approvals, consultation outcomes or a public event.

Piece by Piece is a community mural project located at the Barn Theater at UC Santa Cruz where campus residents and members of the broader Santa Cruz community come together to paint a large-scale mural using pigments made entirely from donated household liquids that have been collected and recycled into natural paint. At the center of the mural stand two figures holding a single torch together and facing forward, one an Awaswas person representing the original stewards of this land and the other a UCSC student representing the living present of the university and the community it inhabits. The Awaswas people spoke a now-extinct Ohlone language and lived in the Santa Cruz Mountains region for over twelve thousand years before colonization, and today their descendants within the Amah Mutsun Tribal Band continue to fight for the restoration of their traditional knowledge and sovereignty over their ancestral homeland. The torch held between the two figures is not meant to suggest that history has been resolved or that harm has been undone but rather that the present moment is an opportunity for people living on this land to face forward together with honesty and with care.

Behind and around the two central figures the mural opens into a sweeping panorama of Santa Cruz itself, and this background is where the project becomes most genuinely participatory. Rather than following a predetermined design, the space surrounding the torch bearers is divided into open sections where participants who show up to paint can contribute their own vision of what Santa Cruz looks and feels like to them. A surfer might paint the morning glass at Steamer Lane while a farmworker from the Pajaro Valley renders the rows of strawberry fields they know by heart, and a student might add the redwood canopy they bike through every morning on the way to class while a longtime local paints the silhouette of the Boardwalk roller coaster against a foggy evening sky. Families walking along West Cliff Drive, musicians busking on Pacific Avenue, the Walton Lighthouse standing at the edge of the harbor, the rolling foothills beyond Pogonip, all of these images emerge not from a single

artist's plan but from the accumulated memory and affection of the people who actually live here. The background becomes a collective portrait of a city painted by the city itself, deliberately dense and layered, refusing to privilege any single version of Santa Cruz over another.

The paint is what deepens this participatory dimension even further. Over several weeks before painting begins, collection stations appear across campus housing communities and at public sites in downtown Santa Cruz where anyone can donate small amounts of household liquids they would otherwise discard. This could include leftover coffee, old cooking oil, wine dregs, expired juice, stale tea, or any safe water-based liquid that carries some color or texture. Student volunteers and local artists then process these donations into usable natural pigments by combining them with binding agents and natural thickeners, and the result is a palette that literally comes from the daily lives of hundreds of different people. Every brushstroke carries the material residue of someone's kitchen, someone's morning routine, someone's particular way of living in this place. The countercultural spirit that has defined Santa Cruz for generations cannot be captured in a single symbol or aesthetic, but a paint palette made from the actual domestic lives of surfers and students and families and farmworkers and longtime locals and newcomers feels like an honest attempt to honor that mix without flattening it.

Weekend painting sessions at the Barn are open to anyone and structured as collaborative gatherings rather than directed labor. Facilitators help maintain the composition around the two central figures but the background belongs to whoever shows up with something to say about this place, and the texture and color of any given section depends on which donated pigments are available and which hands choose to apply them. The group holds a responsibility to ensure that the depiction of the Awaswas figure is developed in consultation with representatives of the Amah Mutsun Tribal Band so that the image carries respect and accuracy rather than appropriation, and this consultation is itself a form of participation that shapes the project long before any paint reaches the wall.

What Piece by Piece hopes to create is not a solution to the complex history of this land but a visible and ongoing conversation about what it means to live here together. Documentation would include photography of each phase, short audio recordings of participants describing what they painted and why, and a published catalog of every liquid that went into the pigments, preserving the communal fingerprint of a project that was never meant to belong to any single person.
