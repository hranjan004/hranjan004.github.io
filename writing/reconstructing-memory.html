Who Gets to Reconstruct Memory?

Morehshin Allahyari, 3D reconstruction, and the uncertainty that a digital model can hide or preserve.

THE QUESTIONWho controls what a reconstruction remembers?

An archived response to Morehshin Allahyari. The title here describes the subject of the originally untitled response.

There is something worth sitting with before accepting any of this too quickly. When an artist claims that fiction can be more truthful than an official record, our instinct might be to nod appreciatively and move on. But I think that idea deserves genuine pressure, because if it holds up under scrutiny, it changes something about how we understand both art and history.

Morehshin Allahyari seems to believe it holds up. Her project Material Speculation was born from a genuinely impossible situation. The Mosul Museum artifacts destroyed by Isis in 2015 were poorly documented even before their destruction, names inconsistent across languages, photographs numbering barely a dozen per object. She used 3D reconstruction anyway, embedding memory cards inside the printed sculptures containing her entire research correspondence, her uncertainties included. What strikes me about this choice is its intellectual honesty. She is not pretending to have recovered the truth. She is building a container for the process of searching for it. That feels meaningfully different from reconstruction as performance of authority.

Her series She Who Sees the Unknown operates on similar logic but reaches further into personal territory, which is where I found myself genuinely pausing. The Laughing Snake stopped me. A monstrous figure who is ultimately destroyed by seeing the reflection men construct for her, and who dies laughing at it rather than in terror. I want to be careful not to over-romanticize this, because there is real grief inside that image. But the reframing of her death as an act of recognition rather than defeat invited me to ask something uncomfortable about how often I accept the mirrors that institutional or social power holds up, not just for others but for myself. That kind of introspective pressure is rare and worth acknowledging.

The Yajooj and Majooj piece worked differently on me. Allahyari describes being stranded in Germany during the travel ban, ten years of built life suddenly contingent on a policy decision made without her in the room. I found myself wondering how many moments of apparent stability in my own life rest on similar contingencies I simply have not encountered yet. That is not a comfortable question, which probably means it is a productive one.

Now, the computer modeling dimension of all this deserves its own honest examination. There is something genuinely liberating about a medium where no decision is permanent until you choose to make it so. Traditional sculpture commits the hand before the mind has fully arrived. Digital modeling allows the artist to hold a form loosely, to return to it, to ask whether it is actually saying what was intended. For Allahyari this is not merely convenient. It is philosophically necessary. Refiguration, her term for reclaiming suppressed mythological figures, requires the ability to revise. It requires treating the past as a living question.

I think what I appreciate most is that none of this work presents itself as having resolved anything. It accumulates questions with great care and leaves them, deliberately, open.
