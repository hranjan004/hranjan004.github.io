Indigenous Rights Under Law: Assimilation and Resistance in China and the United States

This essay compares the legal mechanisms behind formal autonomy and practical subordination. It argues that different constitutional traditions can sustain similar power imbalances, while leaving different openings for resistance.

THE QUESTIONWhen does legal recognition become meaningful autonomy?

Collected Essays · pages 5–9. Original essay text; formatting adapted for the web. Historical claims and interpretations reflect the writing’s original context.

Legal systems justify authority by pointing to texts, institutions, and historical events as sources of "real" law. In John Bell's terms, a source-of-law argument ties an action to an accepted rule or principle within a legal community.¹ Mark Van Hoecke and Mark Warrington treat these sources as embedded in broader "legal cultures"—shared paradigms about what counts as law, who makes it, and why it binds.² This framework helps explain not only what constitutions say, but how they position Indigenous and minority peoples relative to dominant groups.

The United States and the People's Republic of China offer a stark contrast. The U.S. Constitution belongs to a Western legal culture presenting law as autonomous rules limiting government and protecting individual rights, even when practice falls short.² The PRC's 1982 Constitution embeds law in a Leninist paradigm where law openly serves Communist Party policy. Yet both systems structurally subordinate Indigenous and minority peoples. In the U.S., this occurs through rhetoric of "domestic dependent nations" and common-law narrowing of tribal sovereignty; in China, through revolutionary liberation and national unity discourse folding ethnic minorities into a single "Chinese nation" under Party leadership. This essay argues that both countries deploy law to assimilate Indigenous peoples through distinct mechanisms—China through a constitutional façade legitimizing Han Party control, the U.S. through liberal constitutionalism creating friction that enables resistance while perpetuating dispossession. Crucially, in both contexts, Indigenous communities have learned they cannot rely on state institutions alone and must build independent organizations to preserve their cultures, languages, and territorial claims against majoritarian forces.

China: Constitutional Façade and Party Supremacy The modern legal position of China's ethnic minorities grows from Communist Party victory in the 1949 civil war. Mao's proclamation framed the PRC as a "unitary multinational" state jointly built by all nationalities under Party leadership. The 1982 Constitution's preamble and Article 4 constitutionalize this claim, declaring all nationalities equal and promising regional autonomy in minority-concentrated areas. The Law on Regional Ethnic Autonomy (1984) creates "organs of self-government" in autonomous regions, prefectures, and counties. Yet as Haiting Zhang demonstrates, this structure carefully preserves central control: autonomous organs operate strictly "under unified state leadership," and key provisions lack enforcement mechanisms.³

A dual-power structure reinforces this design. Formally, top government posts—governors of Tibet or Xinjiang autonomous regions—must be held by titular minorities. Real authority lies with regional Party secretaries, almost always Han Chinese and not bound by ethnic requirements.³ Courts are not independent arbiters of constitutional rights but administratively subordinate to Party committees; constitutional provisions and the autonomy law are effectively non-justiciable. Law flows downward from the Party and cannot be wielded by minorities to challenge state policy.

Recent legislation makes assimilationist logic explicit. A draft Law on Promoting Ethnic Unity, analyzed by Human Rights Watch, anchors authority in "Xi Jinping Thought" and requires all levels of government, schools, and organizations to cultivate a "strong sense of community of the Chinese nation."⁴ It elevates Mandarin as the common national language, promotes "Chinese cultural symbols," and criminalizes vaguely defined acts "damaging ethnic unity," giving authorities wide latitude to penalize dissent framed as "splittism" or "extremism."⁴

Language and education policy operationalize this vision. Tibet Action Institute's 2021 report estimates 800,000 to 900,000 Tibetan children aged six to eighteen are placed in boarding schools where instruction and daily life are dominated by Mandarin and state-approved narratives.⁵ UN experts warned in 2023 that separating roughly one million Tibetan children from families through such schemes amounts to forced assimilation, undermining their ability to maintain language and culture.⁶

In Xinjiang, this legal culture of "unity through control" produces even starker consequences. The UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights concluded in 2022 that mass arbitrary detention and pervasive restrictions on Uyghurs and other Muslim minorities "may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity."⁷ Constitutional language about equality and autonomy has not limited state power but been folded into a securitized framework treating minority identity as a problem to manage. Within the PRC's legal paradigm, this is not contradiction but function: law operates as a Party-directed instrument for ideological and ethnic homogenization under national unity's banner.

The United States: Constitutional Friction and Subordination The U.S. legal order starts from different constitutional foundations yet also produces subordination. The Indian Commerce Clause and Treaty Clause recognize Indigenous nations as distinct political communities alongside foreign nations and states. The early nineteenth-century Marshall Trilogy translated this recognition into inherent sovereignty coupled with pervasive dependency. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, Chief Justice Marshall called tribes "domestic dependent nations," retaining pre-constitutional sovereignty yet standing in a ward-guardian relationship to the United States. This framework, as N. Bruce Duthu emphasizes, produced a "tripod" of federal Indian law: inherent tribal sovereignty, federal plenary power, and federal trust responsibility to protect tribal interests.⁸

Common-law interpretation and congressional policy steadily tilted this tripod toward domination. In the late nineteenth century, Congress embraced forced assimilation. The General Allotment (Dawes) Act of 1887 broke up communal lands, imposed individual property titles, and opened "surplus" lands to non-Indian settlement, costing tribes roughly two-thirds of their land base.⁸ Assimilationist goals were reinforced through schooling. The Equal Justice Initiative describes federal Indian boarding schools as "cultural genocide" instruments designed to "kill the Indian, save the man" by severing children from language, religion, and community.⁹ Children received English names, faced military regimentation, were punished for speaking their languages, and suffered widespread abuse.⁹

Recent investigations deepen this picture. Between 1869 and 1978, hundreds of thousands of Native children were removed from families; by 1926, an estimated 82 percent of Indian school-age children attended boarding schools or similar institutions.¹⁰ High Country News traces how the flagship Carlisle Indian Industrial School and imitators served as leverage in federal-tribal negotiations, effectively holding children hostage to secure land cessions and policy compliance.¹¹ Law functioned as formal authority—statutes and regulations establishing schools and attendance rules—and rhetorical cover, cloaking coercive assimilation in "civilization" and "education" language.

From the late 1960s, official rhetoric shifted toward "self-determination." Congress passed the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975), allowing tribes to contract with federal agencies to run programs themselves. Courts occasionally recognized robust tribal rights, as in McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), holding Congress never disestablished the Muscogee (Creek) reservation. Yet structural power imbalances persist. The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights' 2018 report Broken Promises concludes federal programs serving Native Americans remain chronically underfunded and poorly coordinated, with appropriations for tribal justice systems meeting only a small fraction of identified needs.¹²

These legal and fiscal structures translate into concrete harms. Amnesty International's Maze of Injustice details how overlapping and conflicting jurisdiction among tribal, federal, and state authorities creates a "maze" frequently leaving Native women without effective protection in sexual violence cases, contributing to dramatically higher victimization rates.¹³ In the resource sector, federal agencies continue prioritizing national economic goals over Indigenous objections. A 2025 joint report by Human Rights Watch and the ACLU on the Thacker Pass lithium mine concludes the Bureau of Land Management approved the project without securing free, prior, and informed consent from the Numu/Nuwu (Northern Paiute) and Newe (Western Shoshone) peoples, whose sacred and burial sites face impact.¹⁴ The rushed consultation and subsequent litigation illustrate how federal plenary power and judicial deference still override tribal claims framed in constitutional and international human rights terms.¹⁴

Comparing Legal Cultures: Façade versus Friction Despite these failures, U.S. legal culture differs from the PRC's in one crucial respect: law is at least partially available as shield and sword. Constitutional and statutory sources justifying dispossession can, in some circumstances, be turned against the state. Tribes, inter-tribal organizations, and Indigenous NGOs invoke treaties, federal statutes, and rights discourse to challenge pipelines, mines, and voting barriers; they lobby Congress, litigate in federal courts, and build their own schools, media, and cultural institutions. Grassroots movements like #NoDAPL at Standing Rock, broader Land Back campaigns, and organizing around Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women draw on legal arguments and moral claims, pressing for fuller self-government and territorial restitution. These efforts don't erase deep injustice in federal Indian law but show the system's internal contradictions create opportunities for Indigenous agency.

For Tibetans, Uyghurs, and Mongols under PRC rule, space for internal legal contestation is much narrower. The dominant legal culture locates sovereignty entirely in the Party-led state and treats ethnic difference as folkloric decoration or security risk. Autonomy provisions lack judicial enforcement; courts implement policy rather than hear minority challenges. Resistance and cultural preservation rely more on informal practices—family transmission of language and religion, local non-compliance where possible—and diaspora activism. Tibetan, Uyghur, and Southern Mongolian organizations based in Europe and North America lobby governments and UN bodies to expose abuses, drawing on documentation like the OHCHR Xinjiang assessment and boarding school reports.⁵ ⁶ ⁷ Their pressure has contributed to sanctions, import bans, and diplomatic censure but hasn't shifted Beijing's core constitutional paradigm.

Conclusion: Independent Institutions as Indigenous Response Neither system offers fully just resolution of Indigenous or minority rights. Both are shaped by territorial consolidation, racial hierarchy, and resource extraction. Yet they differ in how legal cultures mediate this history. In the PRC, Party supremacy fused with collectivist national unity ideology produces a coherent but deeply authoritarian model: law consistently serves as state power's arm to engineer assimilation, and constitutional language about equality and autonomy functions largely as external legitimation. In the United States, individual-rights rhetoric, common-law reasoning, and entrenched settler dominance produce a more contradictory field: tribal sovereignty is simultaneously recognized and eroded, trust responsibility affirmed and starved, and Indigenous communities move in constant push-and-pull between co-optation and resistance.

On balance, the U.S. framework offers somewhat more room for Indigenous peoples to carve out self-governing spaces and use law strategically, even if only partially and unevenly. This limited advantage doesn't excuse historical and ongoing harms but matters for future trajectories. For Native nations, the central challenge is expanding that space—through litigation, political mobilization, and independent institutions—so constitutional promises translate into land, resources, and robust jurisdiction. For minorities in the PRC, the challenge is more existential: keeping languages, religions, and identities alive facing a state openly treating cultural distinctiveness as an obstacle to manage away. In both contexts, Indigenous groups cannot rely solely on constitutional texts or dominant legal culture's goodwill; they must build and defend their own institutions to withstand majoritarian pressures.
