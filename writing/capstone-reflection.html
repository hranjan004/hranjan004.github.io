Building for a Real Home

Scope changes, unreliable labels, sponsor feedback and what it took to keep a real system useful.

My archived Microgrid capstone reflection, with a descriptive title for this collection.

The Microgrid Manager capstone built an energy management system for Dr Mantey's residence in Los Gatos. The pipeline ran from the eGauge meter at the house, through Kafka, into AnyLog, into PostgreSQL, and out through a Grafana dashboard that Dr Mantey could use directly. The eGauge covered three sub panels of the home. Panel 1 handled HVAC including the heat pump and the solar water heater pump, Panel 2 was the standalone water heater, and Panel 3 was the kitchen. The goal was to disaggregate those loads, predict their behavior, and surface recommendations the household could actually act on.

The scope evolved across the six months of the project. Our initial goals were very ambitious and included automation of appliances in Dr Mantey's home, but a three person team can only do so much. We ended up on prediction plus a reactive system tied to the appliances Dr Mantey actually has, and on top of that we built an easy to use dashboard that made the system approachable for non technical users, which became one of the more visible wins.

My role covered the AI models, the research from the papers that fed into our design, and the end to end dashboard. I built the AI model from the ground up, working through different iterations and different training data to see what fit best for the Mantey household. I wrote the rules based system myself and handled the data flow for the household, and my main contributions sat on the infrastructure and the core functioning code. The modeling went through one important pivot. We had started from an LLM based disaggregation approach drawn from the literature, but on live data the labels it produced were unreliable, so I moved to a rule based labeling system that gave clean targets for a BiLSTM trained on Panel 1, with the same approach extending to Panel 2 and Panel 3 through appliance specific rules.

I used Claude as a coding partner throughout. The pattern was that I provided the infrastructure, the step by step functioning, and the initial code blocks for Claude to review, and the tool then helped heavily on the make up for the pre trained AI model and on routine implementation around it. I still wrote the rules based system and the training data myself and ran the training. The pros were speed on well scoped tasks and faster ramp on unfamiliar libraries. The cons were that on genuinely novel problems, like a late stage write blocking bug in our database layer, the tool could not substitute for

actually understanding the system, and it occasionally produced confident but wrong code that required careful review.

The cadence with the sponsor was weekly across the entire six months. Dr Mantey saw progress every week along with the current state of the dashboard, the changes we had made, and ideas we were considering. Roy was part of that weekly review too, and his contribution gave the project its entire breadth. Roy brought deep technical expertise on the data layer, especially on AnyLog, and his input shaped how the pipeline was structured and how we recovered when things broke. Between those weekly reviews with Roy and Dr Mantey, the project had two strong anchors, one on the sponsor side and one on the technical side.

Uncertainty was a constant feature. The largest shift was the move away from automation toward the reactive dashboard. We also had to adjust technology choices when the LLM labeling approach did not hold up on real data, and we adapted again when infrastructure bugs surfaced late in the timeline. Within the team, communication and coordination were straightforward. We met often, reviewed each other's work, and I took more responsibility on the technical side. Decision making was easy at three people, and where I was leading the technical calls I made a point of walking teammates through the reasoning and the results so the direction was clear and shared.

The lesson I am taking away is to keep teammates engaged in the technical work for longer so the load distributes better and so I can direct them into specific parts of the development earlier. This was the most fun I have had in any UCSC class because it had a real sponsor, a real home, and a real system running every day. The change I would suggest is more frequent in person meetings with the professor to stay on track, and more in person meets with the entire team.
