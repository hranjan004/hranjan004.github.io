The problem

Digital logic coursework moved from a stateful counter and display to reaction games with explicit sequencing.

How I approached it

I built a 16-bit loadable up/down counter with seven-segment display output.

Quick Decode and Quick Add extended that foundation with one-hot state machines and LFSR-based logic under structural design constraints.

Earlier labs built an 8-bit ripple-carry adder/subtractor, two’s-complement sign changer, multiplexers and hexadecimal seven-segment decoding. Those components supplied the arithmetic and display foundation for the counter and games.

What changed

Completed the counter and reaction-game labs, building the synchronous design foundation used later in the Osmosis VGA game.

What I took from it

These labs taught me to reason in clock edges and state transitions. That foundation carried into the larger Osmosis design, where counters, timing and display logic have to cooperate continuously.

Engineering decisions

The counter lab exercises registered state, direction and load control. The reaction games add state sequencing and pseudo-random behavior; Osmosis extends the progression into display timing and moving objects.
